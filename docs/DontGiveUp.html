Don't Give Up

Lead Designer and Programmer







Project Overview



Inspired by Missile Command, Don't Give Up is a short experience about losing everything that you love, friends, family, dreams and passions alike. As you gain more and more you also risk losing more and more in an overwhelming world. With nothing but your weakening cries and a shrinking inner circle, is it possible to stop everything you love from leaving? Can you prevent the inevitable? ​

Don't Give Up is a game I made to reflect how I was feeling and to try and help others process those feelings too. After leaving University and getting a full time job I felt that everything I loved was slowly leaving, friends, passions and dreams were no longer something I could keep close, something I could focus on. The world was overwhelming me and so I created this game as a way to understand that feeling and abstract it into something others could experience and potentially relate to!

Negative and Positive Feedback Loops and Balancing






Throughout development I was reading and studying “The Rules of Play” by Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman. In their book Salen and Zimmerman explore the concept of positive and negative feedback loops and their utilisation in game design. After reading about this concept I decided to go back and analyse the feedback loops I was using in my game and I found that I was mainly using a positive feedback loop for the friend spawning system.

Whilst this was partly intentional to ensure the game felt overwhelming and linked to the narrative themes and game design pillars, it created an unfair experience for players. There was no negative feedback loop to regulate and balance the game system.

After many playtests I found that one way to introduce a negative feedback loop and balance the players experience was to alter the friends’ variables each time the player reached a new state. This change saw me slow friends down as the states increased in order to accommodate for the increasing number of them. I also increased the number required to spawn a friend by 2 each state, so it would take shouting at a friend 8 times before a new one would spawn at the end in comparison to shouting at a friend 4 times before one spawns at the start.

These changes slowed the game down a lot more, giving players a better chance of stopping as many friends as possible from leaving. This balancing change was also reflected in the playtesting feedback I received with players no longer feeling completely hopeless/apathetic and just giving up. The negative feedback loop made players feel like they had more control and that their choices had more impact on how long they survived.


The Death Wave:



Prototype Death Wave Videos:


         

The video on the right is an example of the "step printing" effect found in Wong Kar Wai's "Chungking Express"



The Death Wave was a scrapped idea we had. This system would work similar to storm systems in games like Fortnite. It would be triggered at a random time between 10 and 20 minutes forcing the player to drop everything they were doing to run away and reach a safe point. We also planned to create a "step printing" visual effect that would trigger with the death wave disorienting players and making Death Wave sequences more intimidating.


Originally this was the only form of threat the player would need to face however early in the pre-production stages we realised this system was not only out of our technical scope but would also be difficult to balance and build levels around in 5 months.


We decided to scrap the Death Wave system instead focusing on enemies and their variations. We felt this change was achievable from a scope POV and worked better for balancing gameplay. A small number of enemies can be used a lot easier than the death wave to quickly ramp up pacing and provide the player with a challenge. Furthermore, the enemy system (comprising of three types) was a lot more variable, allowing us to put the player in unique situations over the limiting death wave system.

Starting with the church area, this is where the player starts the game. I did not feel that many changes were necessary, however to improve the tutorial in the beginning I increased the size of the fallen pillars so players could not just walk over them and would instead need to jump over them and learn the jump mechanic before they can move on.

In this room I decided to increase the size of the platforms on the left, ensuring players would actually need to jump instead of just walking up ensuring they understand the mechanic. I also introduced a breakable pot and removed some bars from the window. This window acts as a “zen view” allowing players to see the cube in the next room and thus their objective. I found that introducing the breakable pot and removing the bars increased the chance the player would look through the window driven by the interactability of the pot and the more clear, less obstructed view as well as the blue point light guiding the players eyes to the window due to its contrast with the warmer torch lights.

After:


         



In this room I decided to increase the gap. We teach the player how to swing on ropes here however many players didn't swing and instead just jumped off the rope and onto the platform above. By increasing the distance it forced players to swing, interacting with the mechanic and being taught how it works ready for later sections. I also introduced lights to guide the player upwards and used the shadows/darkness to signal to players they are not meant to go down into the hole.

After:


         



The largest change I made to this room is the removal of the rest point (circled in the image on the left). Rest points act as a major reward for the player, allowing them to save their game and set a checkpoint. I felt that giving it to them this early in the game would not reinforce that. Furthermore, the player needs to have the cube to interact with every other rest point in the game. Having this rest point not require the cube would break the mechanic's rules and cause player confusion.

In the final release we introduced a scripted camera movement to the church rafters sequence. This camera movement allowed us to emphasise the players height, increasing tension and ensuring this section stands out from other areas of the tutorial. Furthermore the camera change clearly showcases the area the player has come from helping create an environment that feels connected and believable

I made a lot of changes to the cube room in the final release. To start, I introduced a cage that the player needs to get the cube out of. From an emotional POV I believe this frames the cube as something the player needs to help, serving the companionship themes of [Don’t] Let Go and ensuring the player feels connected to the cube from the beginning. I also introduced a lever that the player would need to pull to save the cube. This allowed me to create the short puzzle sequence in which the player needs to use everything they have learned in the tutorial (cube throwing, destructible walls, climbing, rope swinging and lever interaction) to free the cube, acting as a final test of the player's understanding of the mechanics.

I also created the cube connection cutscene which helps create a smoother transition between the player not having the cube and suddenly being connected to it. The cutscene also helps establish what the player's objective is.

I removed the ramp from the far side of the room to teach and show players how climbing impacts their stamina as well as teaching them how to reel the cube in since they must let go of the cube in order to climb.

Finally, I moved the purple door that only the cube can unlock to just before the player accesses the outside area. This helps emphasise the outdoor reveal, ensuring the vista feels more rewarding.

I use this initial outside area purely to reward the player with an engaging vista that sets the scene. In the final release I bring the wall down slightly to ensure players notice the tower, once they notice the first tower their camera moves left along the next tower, the bridge and finally to the bridge that is closest to them, moving the player's eyes in a square like motion.

In the final release I removed the rest point from this section, saving it as the player's reward for climbing the tower and ensuring it does not interrupt the vista.

The largest change I made to the outside area is the introduction of an enemy tutorial. In previous versions I introduced enemy combat much later into the game however after playtesting I felt it was necessary to introduce it sooner with a unique enemy that dies in one hit (all others die in two). Having the enemy grab the cube off the pressure plate simultaneously teaches the player how pressure plates work but also teaches them that enemies will grab the cube if it is left unattended. This allows me to then teach the player how to make enemies drop the cube and finally how to defeat them, teaching the player exactly how the enemy works in a step by step process or loop.
