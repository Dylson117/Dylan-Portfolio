Don't Give Up

Lead Designer and Programmer







Project Overview



Inspired by Missile Command, Don't Give Up is a short experience about losing everything that you love, friends, family, dreams and passions alike. As you gain more and more you also risk losing more and more in an overwhelming world. With nothing but your weakening cries and a shrinking inner circle, is it possible to stop everything you love from leaving? Can you prevent the inevitable? ​

Don't Give Up is a game I made to reflect how I was feeling and to try and help others process those feelings too. After leaving University and getting a full time job I felt that everything I loved was slowly leaving, friends, passions and dreams were no longer something I could keep close, something I could focus on. The world was overwhelming me and so I created this game as a way to understand that feeling and abstract it into something others could experience and potentially relate to!

Negative and Positive Feedback Loops and Balancing






Throughout development I was reading and studying “The Rules of Play” by Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman. In their book Salen and Zimmerman explore the concept of positive and negative feedback loops and their utilisation in game design. After reading about this concept I decided to go back and analyse the feedback loops I was using in my game and I found that I was mainly using a positive feedback loop for the friend spawning system.

Whilst this was partly intentional to ensure the game felt overwhelming and linked to the narrative themes and game design pillars, it created an unfair experience for players. There was no negative feedback loop to regulate and balance the game system.

After many playtests I found that one way to introduce a negative feedback loop and balance the players experience was to alter the friends’ variables each time the player reached a new state. This change saw me slow friends down as the states increased in order to accommodate for the increasing number of them. I also increased the number required to spawn a friend by 2 each state, so it would take shouting at a friend 8 times before a new one would spawn at the end in comparison to shouting at a friend 4 times before one spawns at the start.

These changes slowed the game down a lot more, giving players a better chance of stopping as many friends as possible from leaving. This balancing change was also reflected in the playtesting feedback I received with players no longer feeling completely hopeless/apathetic and just giving up. The negative feedback loop made players feel like they had more control and that their choices had more impact on how long they survived.


Level Design Balancing






Going into this project I set the goal of keeping the level design simple to avoid any scope issues since I had to balance working on this game and a full time job. However as development continued and playtests were conducted, it became clear that a lot of the balancing issues would need to be fixed through level design and some changes to the environment.

Every friend attempts to move to one of 8 points on each side of the screen, meaning there are 32 points in total. As a result, friends would spread out across the level, making it much harder for players to catch them all since they were so far apart. This led to the game ending far faster than I intended and left players feeling overly helpless and as though there was no chance of winning or any point in playing at all.

In order to balance their movement I attempted to decrease the number of points the friends could move to. On paper this meant that the friends would all move in similar lines/groups, making it easier for players to manage and stop the friends from leaving. However this solution made the game feel too linear, friends wouldn’t move in a way that felt random so this prototype made the system feel too predictable and got boring quickly.



In my second iteration of this prototype, I kept the 32 points however I introduced environmental hazards. These hazards were programmed in such a way that the friends would avoid them, instead walking around them to create choke points throughout the level. This ensured players could reach the friends easier whilst also enabling them to predict where friends would be instead of attempting to guess their sporadic movement. This balancing change was felt immediately in the subsequent playtest with players reaching higher scores and lasting much longer than they were initially. The addition of learnable movement patterns enforced through restrictions in the environment and choke points ensured players felt like they were in control and could manage the friends easier.

In addition these environmental hazards slow the player down, providing them with the extra challenge of needing to avoid them. This adds some extra conflict to the game system, ensuring it feels more engaging and overwhelming for the player, linking back to the game's themes and design pillars that emphasised stress and an overwhelming tone.



Combo System

The combo system was a feature I added late in the development cycle as a response to a lot of the playtesting feedback I received. Playtesters and myself alike felt there was something missing from the experience, there wasn’t much for the player to interact with other than the friends and the shout ammo flowers. After some research into other incremental and arcade style games I found that a combo system was quite common and decided to begin testing it.



At first the combo system increased player engagement slightly, players felt that there was something else to interact with but it quickly became clear that the combo system didn’t reward them with anything and thus there was not a reason for a lot of players to interact with it. It was an isolated feature that didn’t interact with any other game systems.

To solve this issue I linked the players movement system to the combo system. Each time the player reaches a combo of 10 or a multiple of 10 they would receive a slight speed increase.

This solution worked well in ensuring players felt adequately rewarded and interacted with the system fully which in turn deepened the interactions the player could have with the overall game system.

This feature also balanced the experience and improved the games pacing. Having the player be slow in the beginning because there aren’t enough friends to hit a combo made the beginning state/screen feel a lot more intense and meant that players would move onto state 2 and 3 faster. Furthermore, the combo system increasing the players speed meant that when they are in state 2 and 3 and the friends are clumped into groups they can increase their speed faster to accommodate for the increase in the play/level space, allowing the player to almost balance the scales themself leading to a much more rewarding experience



Shouting Ammo



In early iterations of Don’t Give Up players could shout with no restriction. As a result they were able to keep all of the friends within the circle easily, removing the tension and stress I was attempting to deliver through this experience. Players found the game far too easy in early playtesting builds. As you can see from the video above the player does not need to leave the inner blue circle leading to an extremely easy and unengaging gameplay loop

In order to balance this I first attempted to increase the size of the circle and prevent players from walking within it. This ensured that they could not get close enough to the circle to easily send the friends back and rack up points. However, this solution felt very arbitrary and restrictive, it didn’t add anything to the game system or provide players with something else to interact with. It made the game feel a lot more stale



To solve these problems I decided to limit the players ability to shout at and reset the friends. I looked at Missile Command which served as the primary inspiration for Don’t Give Up and I found that the missile limit feature makes the later screens/levels in Missile Command much more intense. Adding a resource to manage gives the player something else to think about and enrichens the entire system.

This solution worked really well. In the following playtest I found that players were not just hovering around the circle and would instead space themselves out across the level. As a result more friends were able to move outside the circle and the game felt more intense and overwhelming, linking to its central theme and removing the degenerative/exploitative strategy players were previously employing.



Friend Collision and Balancing

Early on in the prototyping phase of the project I decided to remove the collisions on all of the friends to ensure that there were no path finding issues with their AI. However, later in development I felt that the player lacked interaction with the friend system. All the player could do was shout and stun them, limiting the choices they could make and the space of possibility

Attempting to remedy this I created 3 iterations of my game: one with collisions on all friends, one without collisions on any friends and one where the player is slowed down each time they walk through a friend.

I felt that it was important to have the player interact with the friends more to make the game system feel more engaging and as though they were obstacles to the player so I tested each build with some of the playtesters that volunteered.

I found that players did not like the full collision build at all. Many reported that it felt clunky and unnecessarily restrictive leading to a poor player experience. The inverse was also true, players found that the build without any collisions was hollow and quite boring. From a design point of view I also felt that the no collisions build was too easy, players could just run through all of the friends and shout at the ones they wanted to reset, they were not an obstacle or point of conflict for the player.





In the end I decided to continue with the third build where whilst the player is colliding with a friend they will be slowed down. This struck a nice balance between the two builds. It increased the interaction between players and the friends whilst also not slowing the pacing all the way down like the full collision build did. I found that players were more conscious of where the friends were and found the experience slightly more difficult as a result of the need to be more attentive and considerate of the friends' positions. From a narrative design POV I also felt that this addition benefited Don’t Give Up’s themes a lot more, having a friend slow you down and prevent you from saving another one added more depth to the narrative.
